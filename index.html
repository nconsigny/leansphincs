An open, machine-judged competition to find the stateless hash-based signature scheme for post-quantum Ethereum accounts.

DRAFT v0.16 2026-09-10 values in amber are placeholders pending calibration

Two research stages · not open for submissions

Stage 1 is academic research; Stage 2 is the Ethereum selection. Stage 1 searches for one-time signature primitives and measures pure hash work: oracle calls weighted by input bytes / 64, so a construction cannot lower its count by pushing huge inputs, such as whole tree layers, through single calls. Stage 2 builds complete stateless account signatures and applies every deployment gate on the way to an Ethereum choice. Both use the objective in §3: signature bytes times verification work, with signing and keygen work fixed by hard budgets rather than scored. A primitive's score is not a complete scheme's score: composition must re-prove security and account for all setup, signing, authentication and encoding work.

The repository contains a working legacy MVP harness and experimental OTS foundations: exact ranking and counting, byte-level graph evaluation and weighted-query proofs, reconstruction against an oracle-consistent reference, and conditional signing-failure envelopes. No complete OTS security certificate or accepted SPHINCS⁻ baseline #0 exists yet. RISC-V metering, wallet gates, the signing and keygen budget certificates with their comparator binding, external audit and registered verification remain launch work. The current legacy three-file harness still reports |σ| × Hverify, unranked; that is the v0.16 objective's formula, but the harness does not yet certify the budgets.

1Background & motivation

Ethereum's post-quantum roadmap runs on hash-based signatures. The consensus-track scheme is advancing in parallel through the leanSig line of work (Drake, Khovratovich, Kudinov, Wagner; ePrint 2025/055 and the follow-up LeanSig technical note), which builds a generalized XMSS from incomparable encodings, instantiated with Poseidon2 for SNARK-friendliness. On the execution side, SPHINCS⁻ demonstrated the same levers this competition pulls: a traditional hash native to the verification environment (KECCAK256), signature budgets cut from 264 to 214–220, and WOTS+C/FORS+C compression, reaching 3.7 KB signatures verifiable on the EVM for ≈127k gas, without protocol changes. With the August 2026 pivot away from Poseidon toward traditional hash functions (SHA / BLAKE), the design space reopens: the SNARK-friendliness constraint is replaced by a zkVM cost model, and the security argument moves onto much firmer ground: a battle-tested hash modeled as a random oracle.

leanSPHINCS is the name of the scheme this competition exists to find, and its home is the execution layer. leanSig answers the consensus-layer question: how a million validators sign attestations every slot, in a synchronized setting where the signing epoch is part of the scheme. This competition asks the execution-layer question next to it: which signature do quantum-resistant Ethereum accounts use? That question has a different shape, and the constraints in this document are that shape. Statelessness is non-negotiable, because account keys cannot safely keep a counter: users restore from seed phrases and sign from several devices, and for a stateful hash-based scheme a single reused state means a broken key. Key generation and signing are budgeted on hardware-wallet-class devices, because that is where account keys actually live. Verification is priced in zkVM cycles, because execution-layer signatures will be checked inside validity proofs at scale; SPHINCS⁻ told the native-EVM version of this story, and the zkVM cost model is its continuation. The 220 lifetime and the R4 decay rule mirror real wallet usage: a million signatures outlasts almost any account, and heavy accounts degrade gracefully rather than falling off a cliff.

To be explicit about scope: this competition does not pick Ethereum's consensus signature, and it does not compete with leanSig. The two run in parallel on the two layers, sharing techniques, proofs, and people. leanSPHINCS maps how far the stateless design space can be pushed under Ethereum-shaped cost models, and its outputs (schemes, formal proofs, cost frontiers, impossibility results) are evidence for everyone working on the problem, not a verdict.

The competition is an autoresearch competition: it is designed so that autonomous AI research agents can participate on equal footing with human cryptographers. That imposes one discipline on us, the organizers, which turns out to be a virtue: every rule must be machine-checkable. Eligibility and score are decided by a Lean 4 compiler and a deterministic harness, not a jury; humans re-enter only after the leaderboard, in the review gate of §6. "Close to NIST level 1" is not a Lean 4 statement, so §4 makes it one.

2The autoresearch format

The format follows a pattern that recent autoresearch challenges, most directly better.codes (launched August 2026), have already proven: a pinned problem statement, a public frontier repository, and a deterministic promotion gate. Solvers sign in, clone the challenge repo, and work inside a designated submission surface; everything else is pinned. The judging pipeline runs, in order:

  1. Proof check. lake build against a pinned Lean 4 toolchain and dependency set. Zero sorry; axioms restricted to a whitelist; a comparator checks that the exported top-level theorem exactly matches the pinned statement (§4, R7); the scheme's own definitions are the only free part.
  2. Rule audit. Static checks: security-bound extraction and bit-security computation (R2, R3, R4), public-key size (R5), interface conformance (R8).
  3. Metering. The instrumented RISC-V environment (§5) executes every stage and reports the hash, memory, and instruction meters per surface. Execution is fully virtualized and deterministic: identical inputs give identical counts.
  4. Score & promotion. Planned for the frozen stage profile: computed from §3, published with the full CI log. Promotion requires eligibility and either a better score or an improvement to the full size/keygen/signing/verification cost frontier. Current local receipts are unranked and do not promote themselves.

Promoted submissions merge into the public frontier repository, credited to the solver and the model used. The competition is cumulative, not sealed: anyone may build on any promoted submission, and lemmas, proof techniques, parameter dead-ends, and impossibility results are upstreamed so later solvers, human or agent, skip them. Negative results that provably close off part of the design space (e.g. "no parameter choice in this encoding family satisfies R2+R3 below 1.2 KiB") earn credit too.

No human judgment sits in the scoring loop; the review gate of §6 applies after it, between leaderboard and adoption. Humans maintain the rules repository, the assumption registry (R7), and the harnesses, all versioned, with changes applying only to future submissions. The harness itself gets an external audit before launch and a standing bounty for rule exploits: zk.golf's post-launch harness bug, caught by an outside auditor, is the cautionary precedent. The quality bar for this pipeline is the better.codes harness: pinned statement and parameter point, a comparator that accepts only an exactly matching exported theorem, kernel-checked proofs, credited promotion. leanSPHINCS aims to match that bar or raise it. That harness is public and directly reusable: protected target modules, a comparator with a permitted-axiom list, flat size-limited submission roots, in-repo editable baselines, agent-facing instructions, and a rejection explainer.

3Objective

minimize  |σ| × V

both stages: signature bytes × verification work, with signing work S and keygen work K fixed by the R9 budgets (1.5 s signing, 1 minute keygen at the anchor) rather than scored; lower wins, subject to hard security and usability gates

Signing latency and account setup are part of execution-layer UX, but they are bounded needs, not open-ended ones: a signer that answers within 1.5 s and a key setup that finishes within a minute are good enough, and nothing faster buys the account holder anything. v0.16 therefore fixes signing work and keygen work as hard budgets (R9: 1.5 s signing, 1 minute keygen at the anchor) and removes them from the score. The two quantities the network pays for repeatedly, persistent signature bytes and verification work, carry all the optimization pressure. This replaces the v0.14 four-factor objective |σ| × S × V × K1/4 with keygen exponent β = 1/4; the earlier size exponent α = 1.25 is not reinstated, so the score is the plain product.

The hash-work view scores V = Hverify and checks S = Hsign and K = Hkeygen against the R9 budgets, each weighted by input length under the same oracle. A cycle-based view applies the same objective and budgets to registered execution-cost meters. Scores from different profiles or stages are not directly comparable. Keygen includes graph/seed expansion and required setup; signing includes encoding, retries and authentication, and must fit its budget on every execution path; verification includes decoding, padding checks and all authentication work. Delegated work and cache construction cannot simply disappear from accounting.

Compare the exact integer |σ| * V; no roots or floating point are involved. Every scored or budgeted quantity must ultimately be bound to the actual implementation by a certificate. Fixing the budgets also settles the expected-versus-worst-case question for signing: the R9 signing budget is a worst-case cap, so signing work must fit it on every execution path, including all permitted retries, and a signing-failure theorem (R8) is still required. Expected signing work may be reported in the metrics panel but never ranks.

Hash minimization is not a deployment certificate. Polynomial/Reed–Solomon coding, codebook unranking and big-integer arithmetic can save hash calls while adding substantial instructions, memory accesses or table construction. These operations stay in scope when security needs no assumption beyond the ROM, but all executable work must be metered. Keep both hash and cycle frontiers; promotion requires the pinned execution-cost and memory gates, not a claim that arithmetic is free. The cycle profile and caps must be calibrated before awards; they are not yet implemented in the harness.

Fixed-budget search is now the objective itself, not a slice of it. With the signing and keygen budgets fixed, search for the smallest signature-bytes × verification-work product that clears every eligibility gate. Hash chains are not assumed optimal: Emile's exploration identifies regimes where Reed–Solomon-coded constructions can improve that tradeoff. This does not establish global optimality across all ROM-secure schemes.

4Rules

R1–R9 describe the intended complete-scheme Stage 2 gates, not a claim that every gate is implemented today. Stage 1 needs its own pinned one-time game and component budgets (§7); an OTS proof does not certify a stateless account scheme. Eligibility and scoring are separate: no product score compensates for failing a required security, availability, execution-cost or memory gate.

R1Scheme class

The scheme is a stateless hash-based signature: key generation, signing, and verification are deterministic or randomized algorithms whose security reduces only to properties of hash functions modeled as random oracles. No additional algebraic hardness assumptions. Signing keeps no state between signatures (a synchronized/epoch-based variant is a separate track; see OQ-5). The competition's scheme-class policy excludes MPCiTH and VOLEiTH; that policy requires review of the actual construction, not just checking its oracle type (OQ-5).

The scheme is defined in Lean over an abstract oracle (a tweakable hash), so the construction is hash-agnostic by design. Any concrete instantiation is admissible if it is a conventional hash with no exploitable algebraic structure: SHA-2, SHA-3 / SHAKE, and BLAKE3 seed the hash registry, and further hashes join by registry PR under the same governance as R7's assumptions. Arithmetization-oriented hashes built on algebraic structure, Poseidon-style, stay out of scope. Internal digest lengths are the submitter's choice (but see R3's pressure toward 128-bit digests, and the zkVM word-size warning in §9).

Public arithmetic is not an additional hardness assumption. Finite-field operations, polynomial evaluation and Reed–Solomon coding are permitted when the security proof remains ROM-only. Correlated evaluations must be modeled as correlated; a hash-output expansion does not create independent secrets for free. The optional graph-search templates are not an eligibility fence, and their current 128-bit vertex model does not yet implement byte-sized coded leaves or 136-bit seeds.

audit of the actual theorem assumptions and oracle model, plus full execution metering; the oracle type alone is not a syntactic classifier of all allowed constructions

R2Security floor: 124 bits provable, classical ROM

The submission proves, in Lean 4, a concrete SUF-CMA bound in the random-oracle model (strong unforgeability: the adversary must not produce any new valid signature, even for a message that was already signed): for every adversary making at most qH hash queries and qS ≤ 220 signing queries (failed signing requests count toward qS; see R8),

Adv(A) ≤ B(q_H, q_S)

where B is in canonical form: a finite sum of terms c_i · q_H^{a_i} · q_S^{b_i} · 2^{-n·d_i} with explicit rational constants c_i. Define the provable security level λ(B) as the largest λ such that B(qH, qS) ≤ qH / 2λ for all 1 ≤ qH ≤ 2λ and qS ≤ 220. Require λ(B) ≥ 124. The floor sits deliberately below what is now known to be reachable: 127 bits are machine-checked for synchronized XMSS (OQ-1). Treat 124 as a floor, not a target.

bound extracted from the Lean statement; λ(B) computed mechanically by the rule auditor

R3Idealized bound: 128 bits with constants dropped

Let B̃ be B with every constant c_i replaced by 1. Require λ(B̃) ≥ 128.

This is the SPHINCS+ convention made mechanical: the r3.1 specification computes bit security while "neglecting the small constant factors inside the logarithm" (§7.1.1). R2+R3 together land submissions where NIST level 1 in practice lands: a real proof within a few bits of 128, where the missing bits are exactly the log-of-constant losses that every accepted security argument in this literature absorbs. Because the canonical form makes "the constants" a syntactic object, dropping them is a computation, not a judgment call.

same auditor as R2, run on B̃

R4Slow decay: security beyond the rated lifetime

Re-evaluating the same bound at qS ≤ 232 must give λ(B) ≥ 100. A key pushed 4096× past its rated lifetime degrades gracefully instead of falling off a cliff.

same auditor as R2, with q_S = 2^32

R5Quantum security: heuristic, not proved in-competition

No QROM proof is required. The working heuristic is quantum security ≈ classical security / 2, giving ≥ 62-bit QROM at 220 signatures and ≥ 50-bit at 232 from R2/R4; this meets the 64-bit target up to the same constant-factor tolerance R3 grants classically. A QROM proof for the winning scheme is a post-competition deliverable of the organizers, not the entrants. Requiring it in-competition would multiply the formalization burden for every entrant to certify a bound we expect to hold generically for ROM proofs of this shape.

none (follows from R2/R4 by the stated heuristic)

R6Public key ≤ 64 bytes

The serialized public key is at most 64 bytes. (Keys are meant to live on-chain; a Merkle root plus parameters fits comfortably.)

static check on the reference implementation's serialization

R7End-to-end Lean 4, with a governed assumption registry

The R2 theorem is proved in Lean 4 in VCVio, on a statement layer built over its HashSig library (OQ-1). The statement/proof separation follows the XMSS formalization on leanVM-b's xmss-fv branch: the pinned statement lives in one file a reviewer can read without proof machinery, the claim is discharged in a root module, and the axiom footprint is pinned with #guard_msgs so the build fails if it ever grows. Two proof styles are admissible, and the rules must not privilege either:

  • Direct ROM arguments: the bound proved outright against the random oracle.
  • Modular reductions (2025/055-style): the scheme reduces to named idealized-hash properties (multi-target undetectability, collision-resistance variants, …), each of which carries a normative attack-complexity bound.

For the modular style, terminal assumptions must come from the assumption registry: a versioned list in the rules repo, each entry a property with its assigned generic-attack bound (proven in the ROM where possible, conjectured with cryptanalytic justification otherwise). Registry additions go through a human-reviewed PR, the one deliberate exception to full automation, because an entrant must not be able to invent a conveniently strong assumption. The registry is seeded from the properties and bounds of ePrint 2025/055 and the SPHINCS+ line of analysis.

lake build, zero sorry, axiom footprint pinned via #guard_msgs, assumption set ⊆ registry

R8Interface conformance

Intended full API: keygen(seed) → (pk, sk, cache), sign(sk, cache, msg) → σ or ⊥, verify(pk, msg, σ) → bool, plus an optional non-secret presign stage (§5.3). Signing may explicitly fail (return ⊥): bounded-attempt grinding and target-sum encodings need a way to give up without emitting an invalid signature or looping forever. The implemented claim has two separate obligations: successful outputs verify with probability one; and, for each fixed message, the failure probability over fresh key generation, signing randomness and the shared oracle is at most 2−128. A stronger certificate such as 2−256 qualifies. This is a per-message, fresh-key gate, not an adaptive lifetime-availability guarantee; it does not move the 124-bit floor and adds no scored metric.

In the implemented SUF-CMA game a failed request is visible and consumes signing budget; only a successful message-signature pair can count as a replay. Exact signature size covers successful outputs. The full game must additionally expose the presign transcript and make the cache adversary-replaceable (R9, §5.2); those channels are not in the legacy claim. Messages are 32 bytes, verification is deterministic given oracle answers, and serialization must be byte-exact. The intended reference implementation targets RISC-V, with no_std Rust on the wallet side and a proved connection to the Lean scheme.

Worst-case failure analysis is separate from worst-case signing work. The experimental adaptive failure envelope requires a conditional success/failure-mass premise in the actual ROM/cache game, with explicit allowances for exceptional cached steps. A 2−128 single-request allowance does not establish 2−128 failure over an entire lifetime. The stronger adversarial/lifetime availability profile remains to be pinned and proved; no such theorem is inferred from codebook size alone.

conformance test vectors plus the corresponding Lean correctness and availability obligations; auxiliary-channel game integration remains launch work

R9Wallet-side budgets

On the wallet cost vector (§5.2): key generation ≤ 1 minute and signing ≤ 1.5 s at the 160 MHz anchor, signer RAM ≤ 64 KiB (the user RAM of the Ledger Nano S+ secure element), plus a persistent non-secret cache of ≤ 4 KiB, written at key generation and readable at signing (§5.2). The caps bind the wallet-side stages only; delegated pre-signing has its own budget (§5.3).

v0.16 fixes these as the competition's work budgets: signing stays at 1.5 s, key generation moves from 45 s to 1 minute, and both leave the score (§3). The 5-second signing / 5-minute keygen proposal was not adopted. In the hash-work view the budgets read S ≤ 8.5 × 104 and K ≤ 3.4 × 106 hash-work units, the anchor's hash-only equivalents (§5.2); the cycle-based profile, once pinned, gates on metered execution cost instead. Hard limits must include encoding, finite-field and big-integer operations, allocations, table preparation and all permitted retries. Failing before the deadline does not waive R8's availability gate. A hash-only score or average timing run is not a worst-case latency certificate.

required wallet execution/memory gates (§5.2); not enforced by the current legacy MVP harness

Trick categories and proof templates

The proof burden should not restart from zero for every submission. The plan: the rules repo categorizes the known SPHINCS-family optimization tricks and ships a Lean-proved security template per category, built on HashSig. Submissions declare which categories they compose and instantiate the templates; a genuinely new trick enters by contributing a new template through the same registry governance as R7. The seed taxonomy, drawing on the Blockstream Research SPHINCS-Parameters work:

  • Grinding / compression: WOTS+C, FORS+C (signer retries buy shorter signatures).
  • Checksum elimination: target-sum Winternitz.
  • Authentication-path compression: octopus-style multi-path batching, stripping redundant nodes across the few-time layer's Merkle openings.
  • Few-time layer choice: FORS vs. PORS+FP.
  • Tree shape: hypertree layering, tall cached top trees (R9's 4 KiB cache).
  • Encodings: incomparable and hypercube encodings from the leanSig line.

The intended payoff is reproducibility: a pinned binary, input, oracle-answer trace and metering profile produce the same counts. A new compiler or profile is a new comparison context, not silently interchangeable evidence. The target is to bind both score factors and every work and resource gate to the actual program. Today only the verification metric and experimental graph schedules have the corresponding weighted-query proofs; full cycle metering and the signing and keygen budget certificates remain to implement.

A hash-work unit is not a concrete compression block or an instruction, even though the input unit is now 64 bytes. Retain query lengths and domain-separation layout: concrete-hash padding, initialization, output expansion and the chosen hash affect execution cost. A fixed multiplier on the aggregate hash-work count is not generally an exact cycle conversion. The concrete-hash profile must account for that work once, without also double-charging instructions used to implement the same hash.

The riscv-zkvm interpreter is the intended foundation. Oracle integration, metering and the connection between the Lean scheme and its executable still need implementation and proof. A verified trace would certify that execution under the selected model; it would not alone prove a worst-case bound over all inputs, randomness and oracle histories.

5.1The verification views

The zkVM view prices what Ethereum pays to SNARK-aggregate signatures; its weights are calibrated from published zkVM cost data rather than tied to any single zkVM release. Anchor: a production accelerator table prices SHA-256 at 68 cycles per 64-byte block (+6 init), and precompiles generally cut hashing 5–10× versus pure RV32IM. Fixing the exact weights, with a published rationale per registered hash, is OQ-2.

Published compression-call figures are useful context, but do not automatically equal the competition's block-weighted Hverify. They must be re-metered under the same oracle, input encoding and domain-separation convention before comparison. Encoding and field arithmetic must also be included in the execution view.

Hash counts can inform an EVM gas estimate, but miss arithmetic, decoding, memory and other costs. Likewise, the native RISC-V row is a proposed one-cycle/one-gas comparison profile, not an assertion about an adopted Ethereum gas schedule. Concrete deployment claims require the corresponding executable and pinned pricing model.

Following zk.golf, the goal is certified quantities, not optimistic timing samples. A full claim must bind exact serialization, V and the budgeted S and K to the implementation, bound signing work in the worst case, and certify the chosen execution profile. Worst-case bounds must include malformed verification inputs and retry exhaustion. A measured average can inform engineering but cannot replace those proof obligations.

5.2The wallet vector (secret path)

The wallet model retains the 160 MHz MCU anchor and published SHA-256 software benchmark of approximately 44 cycles/byte as calibration inputs, not a certified conversion for every hash or program. The 64 KiB RAM budget was chosen from the Ledger Nano S+ secure-element profile (ST33K1M5C). No hardware hash accelerator is assumed (OQ-3). R9's time budgets correspond arithmetically to 9.6 × 109 anchor cycles for key generation and 2.4 × 108 for signing. A calibrated execution model, device validation and direct memory high-water accounting are still required; the current harness does not enforce these wallet limits.

The keygen budget admits designs that do real one-time work at key setup (roughly 3.4 million hash blocks of headroom); the signing budget leaves comfortable margin for grinding-style tricks. The RAM cap is the binding constraint: 64 KiB forces streaming Merkle computation instead of large in-memory state. The cache is what makes tall top trees affordable to sign with: in a height-14 top layer, keygen can store all 128 depth-7 digests (128 × 32 B = 4 KiB), and signing then rebuilds one height-7 subtree plus the cheap upper path, 27 leaf derivations instead of 214. A square-root saving, paid once at keygen.

The intended auxiliary-channel game lets the adversary read and replace the cache. A security proof must cover the actual disclosure and replacement behavior; “non-secret” is not established by naming it a cache. Authentication, rejection or recomputation must be charged to the execution budget. Availability under cache corruption needs its own explicit policy; the current fresh-key failure theorem does not establish it. This game extension is not implemented in the legacy claim.

5.3The host vector (non-secret pre-signing)

Splitting the signer in two is where the unified environment earns its keep. In hash-based schemes much of the signer's work is not secret at all: Merkle tree assembly over already-derived leaf public keys, auth-path maintenance, and similar bookkeeping can run on an ordinary computer. The interface therefore includes an optional presign stage (R8), invoked at key setup or ahead of a signature, that runs on the host surface with a generous budget.

The intended security game exposes the presign stage's entire input/output transcript to the adversary. This must be incorporated into the theorem before delegation is eligible; the current legacy game has no presign channel. The profile must also specify how host work is accounted against the fixed signing and keygen budgets, alongside its separate host budget. Neither secrecy nor a zero-cost setup exemption follows from calling work non-secret.

6Scoring, submissions, leaderboard

  • Shared objective = |σ| × V, with signing and keygen work held to the fixed R9 budgets. Compare the exact integer |σ| * V; lower wins. This applies separately within each stage and frozen metering/signing profile, subject to its hard gates.
  • Metrics panel: retain size; hash work for keygen, signing and verification; corresponding RISC-V instruction/memory costs; RAM and cache high-water marks; and signing-failure/work bounds. Encoding, polynomial arithmetic and codebook unranking are included in execution cost, not hidden behind a hash-count improvement.
  • Re-rankable: preserve the full frontier and reproducible raw evidence. Show the hash-work and registered cycle-based products separately. Stage 1 exposes this as two tabs: Spacetime, the |σ| × V ranking, and Pareto, the size/verification frontier at the fixed budgets; Stage 2 follows the same layout once it opens. Selection of the final prize profile is pending calibration and review; no award follows from an incomplete hash-only experiment.
  • Promotion: once open, an eligible submission may improve the profile's score or its full cost frontier. Published records must state stage, game, metric semantics, profile and verifier provenance. The current unranked receipt or an experiment JSON cannot promote itself.
  • Review gate: topping the CI leaderboard does not make a scheme the scheme. Automated checks decide eligibility and rank; a manual review by the organizers and the leanSig authors stands between the leaderboard and any award or adoption, to keep out submissions that satisfy the letter of the rules while missing their point. Review outcomes are published with reasons.
  • Credit: every promotion is credited to the solver and the model used; the leaderboard publishes the score history, improvement-per-model, and the live Pareto frontier. The rules are the same for humans and agents; that is the point of making them mechanical.
  • Ties break toward the smaller signature first (size outlasts verification cost; §3), then by earlier submission timestamp; a re-submission resets the timestamp.
  • Reproducibility: the CI pipeline is the ground truth; anyone can re-run it. Scores are published with full logs and the exact harness commits.
  • Rules bounty: exploits of the harness, the comparator, or the rule text are in scope, credited on the leaderboard, and patched with a rules-version bump.

7Two stages and the legacy MVP

Stage 1 · primitives, not just WOTS

Stage 1 is the academic research track: it measures pure hash work, oracle calls weighted by input bytes / 64, so huge inputs cannot hide work in a single call. Search for the best provably secure OTS primitives and retain a full cost frontier: chains, target-sum/hypercube encodings, branching graphs, shared seeds and ROM-secure coded constructions. Optional proof templates accelerate search; an open direct-proof route must admit constructions beyond those templates. “Best found” is not “globally optimal”: a lower-bound result must name the precise family and cost model it covers. The Stage 1 board has two tabs. Spacetime ranks primitives by signature bytes × verification work under the fixed signing and keygen budgets; Pareto shows the full size/verification frontier at those budgets, so a primitive that is not the product winner but dominates a region stays visible. Both tabs share one metering profile and never mix profiles.

The initial target is strong one-time unforgeability, with at most one signing request, plus correctness, exact canonical serialization, cost bounds and signing availability. The precise standalone chosen-message versus internal-root signing game remains to be agreed with the construction authors. Multi-instance security and same-message repeat-signing behavior need explicit composition contracts; none is inferred from a single-instance OTS theorem. Stage-specific primitive signing and keygen budgets, fractions of the full-scheme R9 budgets, must also be pinned before eligibility opens.

Stage 2 · complete stateless account signatures

Stage 2 is the Ethereum selection track. Compose promising primitives with few-time components, hypertrees and authentication-path compression, then re-prove the complete stateless SUF-CMA game and measure every lifecycle stage. Use the same objective, the full-scheme R9 work budgets and the other hard gates. Baseline #0 is intended to be a SPHINCS⁻ variant satisfying the competition claim, not a metric canary or a standalone OTS. Emile's formal SPHINCS line is the leading integration route; its security proof is not yet a serialized, cost-certified competition entry.

What the existing phase-0 harness actually checks

The legacy MVP remains an engineering test profile, not either new leaderboard:

  1. Classical ROM claim. The protected claim uses the 124-bit floor at 220 signing requests, correctness on success, a separate fresh-key signing-failure gate, exact signature size and the public-key cap. It does not enforce the idealized companion, lifetime decay or QROM rules.
  2. One oracle, no named hash. Variable-length input, N B → 32 B. The implemented verification meter now charges ceil(inputBytes / 64) per call, including supplied domain-separation bytes, as pinned in §5. The security game separately counts raw whole-experiment queries, including empty inputs.
  3. Legacy score. The three declared files remain sigma.txt, hverify.txt and bound.txt. Local success reports |σ| × Hverify, explicitly unranked; that product is now the objective's formula, but the protected comparator does not yet bind the signing and keygen budget certificates. Do not submit additional metric files as though they were accepted.
  4. Isolation, not deployment certification. The Linux sandbox, source capture, comparator, axiom audits, content-bound receipts and admission lock are implemented. RISC-V metering, wallet/presign/cache gates, signed verifier attestation and production registration are not.
  5. No cryptographic baseline yet. Positive comparator fixtures prove only metric statements. Side-channel and fault-resistance certification also remains outside this engineering profile.

Wallet-side caps (R9) and presign/cache requirements stay defined for Stage 2 but are not gating in the legacy MVP. Full eligibility must not inherit that exemption. See the implemented submission contract for what can actually be verified today.

9Design space: known tricks, open frontier

A non-exhaustive map for entrants, human or agent, of where the known wins and open conjectures sit:

  • Encodings. Target-sum Winternitz kills the checksum chains; incomparable encodings generalize; the hypercube / top-layer encodings of 2025/1332 buy signature size at the same security. The encoding is the highest-leverage free variable.
  • Chain-count margin. There is a known (unpublished) attack putting standardized SPHINCS+ level 1 strictly below 128 bits provable. Conjecture on the table: +2 WOTS chains at chain length 8 recovers provable 128, a cheap fix if it holds. The rejected alternative, 129-bit digests, would poison SNARK aggregation; don't.
  • Tighter proofs over bigger parameters. SPHINCS+-style bounds give ≈124.5 bits from 128-bit digests. If the R2/R3 pair is winnable with a tighter proof at smaller parameters, that beats padding the scheme. The 127-bit conjecture is no longer just a conjecture: leanVM-b's xmss-fv branch carries a machine-checked 127-bit classical theorem for a concrete synchronized target-sum XMSS. The open question is transporting that tightness to the stateless setting.
  • Grinding. WOTS+C-style grinding at signing time trades signer cycles (budgeted by R9) for signature bytes (scored). The R9 budget is deliberately the knob that bounds this trick. Bounded attempt counts are also why signing may fail explicitly; R8's 2−128 availability gate is what bounds that failure.
  • Delegation-friendly signing. The presign split (§5.3) rewards schemes whose heavy bookkeeping is provably non-secret: the more of the signer that runs on the host under the adversary-visible-transcript rule, the less pressure on the 64 KiB / 1.5 s wallet budget. Designing so the delegated transcript is trivially simulatable is a new axis this cost model opens.
  • Keygen-time caching. The 4 KiB non-secret cache (R9) generalizes to a pebbling knob: storing the 2d nodes at depth d of a height-h top tree makes signing cost 2h−d leaf derivations, so cache size trades directly against signing time. Because the cache is adversary-replaceable in the security game, the trick is only available where tampering is provably harmless, which top-layer digests are.
  • Polynomial-coded disclosures. Reed–Solomon-style evaluation of hash-derived coefficients can enlarge the disclosure design space. Such values are correlated, and enough evaluations permit interpolation. Prove the actual leakage/composition properties; charge polynomial evaluation, field operations and decoding in the cycle profile. A hash-only improvement is not an execution-speed result. See the bounded review of the supplied annex; it is not an accepted construction or security certificate.
  • zkVM-shaped verification. Keep digests within word-multiple sizes; batch hash calls into precompile-friendly blocks; structure Merkle paths for sequential memory access. The instruction mix is visible in the cycle-based view and mandatory execution gates even when the research score uses hash work.
  • Multi-target tightness. The multi-target/multi-instance losses are where classical proofs bleed constants; this is where R3's forgiveness is spent, and where a clever proof reclaims it.

10Open questions

OQ-1 · Proof-stack details

VCVio remains the proof framework. The protected legacy statement now implements the shared-ROM SUF-CMA game, canonical-bound endpoint theorem, correctness, signing-failure and weighted-verification obligations. HashSig's concrete-function components still need the appropriate oracle-based connection for reuse. The locally reproduced PR #19 stateless SPHINCS reference proves a 126-bit statement at its pinned revision with only standard axioms; synchronized XMSS provides a separate 127-bit reference. Neither is automatically an eligible entry. Serialization, game transport, K/S/V certificates, availability, the Stage 1 composition contract and full-program metering remain integration work. R3 additionally needs a fixed normalization convention before its idealized bound can become a protected gate.

OQ-2 · Cost-vector values

The objective is |σ| × V with fixed signing and keygen budgets in both stages (v0.16). Remaining choices are the Stage 1 budget fractions, concrete instruction/memory/hash weights, execution and host-presign caps and the final award profile. Keep the hash and cycle views separate, and re-optimize parameters under each. No single 64-byte-input/64-byte-output toy oracle fixes the practical winner. Changes are versioned and apply only to future submissions.

OQ-3 · Wallet model details

The R9 budgets are set (1 minute keygen, 1.5 s signing, 64 KiB RAM). Two details remain: whether a hardware SHA accelerator may be assumed (it roughly halves effective cycles per byte and exists in this MCU class), and whether the time budgets should additionally be validated on a secure-element profile, since the Nano S+ runs its Cortex-M35P at 70 MHz and wall-clock there is roughly 2.3× the 160 MHz model.

OQ-4 · Provable bits in the score

Suggestion from the Berlin thread: make provable security a scored dimension rather than a gate, e.g. dividing the score by a function of λ(B) − 124, so tighter proofs are rewarded, not merely admitted. This couples the score to proof style; it remains parked and is not part of the objective.

OQ-5 · Scope boundaries

The cryptographic fence concerns assumptions, not whether a program performs algebra. Public finite-field coding may be admissible with a ROM-only proof; correlated polynomial evaluations must not be treated as independent hash outputs. The oracle type alone does not enforce the complete scheme-class policy. The pure-ROM scope excludes constructions requiring additional hardness assumptions. A second proof under weaker standard assumptions and a synchronized track remain separate open options.

OQ-6 · Signature size soft cap

Should |σ| carry a hard cap near single-packet size? The safe UDP payload figure is 1232 bytes (IPv6 min MTU 1280 − headers; discv5 caps packets at 1280 B), but note consensus gossip does not actually impose it. The score already pressures size; a hard cap may be redundant. Leaning: no cap, keep the objective clean.

OQ-7 · Expert review

R2/R3 thresholds (124/128) and the registry seed need sign-off from the leanSig authors, pending Benedikt's return from vacation. Emile's attack (SPHINCS+ level 1 below 128 provable) should be written up and cited as the motivating example for why R2 ≠ 128. The xmss-fv result, 127 machine-checked bits for synchronized XMSS, adds a fresh question for that review: whether the R2 floor should move from 124 toward 127.

OQ-8 · Hash work versus execution cost (reopened)

The objective is shared; the definitive deployment/prize profile is not frozen. Polynomial coding and expensive codebook arithmetic show why a small hash-count saving can hide an execution regression. Retain a hash-work research view, add the cycle-based view, and require hard execution/memory gates. Concrete weights, caps, full-program cost proofs and a baseline comparison are required before claiming a practical winner.
